• The First Chaser: David Hoadley is considered the world's first systematic storm chaser. He began tracking storms in North Dakota in 1956, using data from local airports and weather offices instead of modern radar. • Project VORTEX: Launched in 1994, this was the largest field research project in history dedicated to studying tornadoes. It completely revolutionized our understanding of how supercell storms form twisters. Science & Technology • The Hook Echo: Chasers look for a distinct "hook" shape on reflectivity radar. This hook indicates that rain and hail are wrapping around the storm's updraft, which is a primary indicator that a tornado is forming or already on the ground. • The "Bear's Cage": This is chaser slang for the area between the main updraft and the heavy rain/hail core of a supercell. Entering the bear's cage is incredibly dangerous because visibility drops to zero, and a tornado could be hidden right next to you. Vehicles & Gear • Tornado Intercept Vehicles (TIVs): Real-world chasers (like Sean Casey and Reed Timmer) built heavily armored vehicles like TIV-2 and the Dominator. These vehicles feature bulletproof glass, armor plating, and hydraulic spikes that anchor them into the ground to withstand 200+ mph winds. • The "Core Punch": This is a tactic where chasers drive directly through the heaviest rain and hail core of a storm to reach the tornado on the other side. It risks severe vehicle damage from giant, baseball-sized hail.
